## From Embodied Awareness to Correspondent Agency, Decidability, and Natural Law

**By: Brandon Michael Hayes** President, Natural Law Institute

## Executive Summary

The supposed hard problem of consciousness persists because we have assigned consciousness the functions, complexities, and significance of capacities that are not consciousness. Sensation, perception, awareness, attention, memory, reasoning, intelligence, self-representation, agency, and moral judgment are related achievements of biological organization, but they are not interchangeable. When these distinct phenomena are compressed into a single explanatory category, consciousness becomes an impossibly complicated object whose apparent mystery is largely manufactured by our failure to classify it correctly.

**Consciousness is embodied awareness: a natural capacity arising within an already functioning organism, integrating experience with the biological requirements of adaptive behavior.** It is not the origin of life, the source of intelligence, the substance of personal identity, or the foundation from which moral worth and lawful authority can be independently derived. It is one capacity within a recursive hierarchy of physical, biological, cognitive, and behavioral organization.

The evidence is available in ordinary experience. A newborn feels hunger and discomfort before acquiring language or sophisticated reasoning. A dog experiences its environment, learns, anticipates consequences, and responds without possessing human powers of abstraction. Sleep, dreaming, anesthesia, hypnosis, hallucination, and other altered states demonstrate that awareness, behavioral performance, memory, and accurate representation of reality can vary independently. These distinctions do not make consciousness unreal. They make its location within the causal organization of living beings increasingly intelligible.

The central philosophical mistake is to treat the difference between experiencing a phenomenon and describing its physical operation as evidence that something additional must exist beyond the physical organization. A difference in access, perspective, or explanatory completeness does not itself establish a separate metaphysical object. The physiological mechanisms producing particular experiences remain legitimate subjects of scientific investigation, but an incomplete explanation does not warrant inventing an irreducible substance or property to fill the gap.

**The consequential problem is not whether an organism experiences reality. It is whether the organism's representations correspond to reality sufficiently to guide competent action.**

A conscious agent can be mistaken. An intelligent agent can be mistaken. An educated, confident, and sincere agent can construct an elaborate explanation that fails to correspond to the world. Human cognition makes these failures especially consequential because language, memory, social reinforcement, and institutional authority allow defective representations to persist long after their errors become discoverable.

The real developmental achievement is therefore the progression from awareness through learning, prediction, reasoning, and increasingly sophisticated agency toward **correspondent agency**: the capacity to distinguish what is observed from what is inferred, correct representations against evidence, select among available actions, and account for the actual consequences produced.

This is where the Natural Law Institute's broader program enters.

By disambiguating phenomena, enumerating relevant agents and constraints, serializing causal dependencies, and requiring correspondence between claims and reality, we can extend the same scientific discipline from neurobiology into psychology, cooperation, competition, exchange, law, and institutional organization. The question shifts from what people believe, intend, or profess to what their knowledge and conduct demonstrably accomplish, what costs they impose, and whether those consequences are permissible among independently capable agents.

This produces a higher floor of **decidability**. Where information is available, claims can be tested. Where information is missing, the deficit can be identified. Where uncertainty remains, it can be bounded. Where institutional functions fail, the errors can be located and corrected. Increasingly powerful artificial intelligence makes this discipline more urgent, because accelerating decisions without corresponding improvements in verification may accelerate existing errors rather than eliminate them.

Artificial consciousness consequently becomes an engineering question concerning physical organization, embodiment, integration, and adaptive regulation—not a reason to preserve metaphysical mystery. Whether alternative substrates can reproduce the relevant organization remains a matter for demonstration. And even genuine artificial consciousness would not automatically establish competence, moral authority, or a right to govern human affairs.

The same accounting extends to civilization itself. Natural law concerns preserving the conditions under which capable agents can emerge, develop, cooperate, act independently, and recover from catastrophe. The biological, ecological, familial, educational, and institutional foundations of human agency must remain sufficiently resilient that civilization can survive even the failure of its own technologies and governing structures.

The accompanying analysis places the contributions of Chalmers, Nagel, Dennett, Bach, Gómez-Emilsson, Levin, the Damasios, and contemporary consciousness researchers within their respective causal domains. Their discoveries need not be dismissed to reject the proposed metaphysical remainder. They become more useful when their findings are assigned to the particular phenomena they illuminate.

**The objective was never to make everyone more conscious.**

It is to make conscious beings increasingly capable of understanding reality, correcting their errors, governing their choices, producing value, cooperating reciprocally, and preserving the conditions under which others can do the same.

The Natural Law Institute proposes that the path from embodied awareness to natural law is one continuous causal investigation. Correctly locate consciousness near the beginning of that hierarchy, and the increasingly consequential questions of knowledge, agency, responsibility, and civilization become available for systematic scientific treatment.

**We are establishing the floor of decidability, returning the burden of explanation to causal reality and the burden of action to demonstrated competence.**

## I. Consciousness Is Not Particularly Mysterious to Those Who Work With It

For many years, I bred German Shepherds and trained dogs. I have spent an extraordinary amount of time working with animals whose consciousness is not in question.

A dog smells something, recognizes it, anticipates what follows, experiences excitement or apprehension, and selects a response. It remembers previous encounters, learns from consequences, becomes frustrated when something it expects fails to occur, can be afraid of something that presents no actual danger, or perfectly comfortable in circumstances it has misunderstood.

Training involves understanding how those capacities operate and how they can be developed.

The dog is conscious before the training begins. Training does not make it conscious. It alters the quality of the animal's representations, expectations, discrimination, and behavioral responses.

I can improve a dog's ability to identify a signal, distinguish relevant from irrelevant information, inhibit an impulse, anticipate consequences, and cooperate with a human handler. What I am developing is not consciousness itself, but increasingly correspondent behavior through capacities built upon consciousness.

- A conscious dog can be wrong.
- A conscious dog can possess accurate sensory information and still select an ineffective response.
- A conscious dog can perform an extraordinarily sophisticated learned behavior without consciously deliberating over each component.
- A conscious dog can be frightened by a false expectation, frustrated by a misunderstood signal, or confident in a response that will produce an undesirable consequence.

We distinguish these failures by investigating the organism, its sensory apparatus, its learning history, its environment, and the relationship between its expectations and the actual world.

Yet, we never invoke metaphysics, nor do we have difficulty recognizing that a dog possesses consciousness merely because it cannot provide a philosophical explanation of its own experience.

Much of the contemporary consciousness debate begins from the opposite direction, with the extraordinary cognitive capacities of mature human beings, then, associates those capacities with consciousness, and subsequently struggles to explain how consciousness could possibly possess all the complexity attributed to it.

It does not. The complexity belongs to the organization of the animal and to the additional capacities developed throughout its life.

**Consciousness has become mysterious because we have assigned to it the functions, significance, and value of things that are not consciousness.**

Restore the causal distinctions, and the mystery rapidly diminishes.

The more consequential question is what conscious beings become capable of doing, how accurately they understand their circumstances, and whether their choices produce the consequences they intend.

That is where the real work begins.

## II. The Error Is Conflation

Consciousness is embodied awareness mediating between sensory functions, internal processing, and the selection of adaptive responses.

It is a capacity arising within an organism whose physical organization already exists, already regulates itself, and already depends upon interaction with its environment.

Consciousness is not the origin of the organism.

It is not intelligence, reasoning, memory, personality, autobiographical self-representation, or moral agency, nor is consciousness the substance from which the value of a living being is derived.

These capacities are related, but relationship does not establish identity.

Sensation registers differences. Perception organizes them. Conscious awareness makes aspects of those conditions available within the organism's integrated experience. Attention allocates priority. Memory retains consequences. Prediction anticipates possible futures. Reason enables increasingly explicit comparison. Agency permits increasingly sophisticated action within constraints.

Personality and psychological economy organize the resulting capacities into persistent patterns of behavior, expectation, attachment, and motivation across time.

When we compress these distinctions into a single word, consciousness, we create an explanatory object that no longer corresponds to any one thing in nature.

Then we ask why this invented object is so difficult to explain.

We have manufactured a problem through misclassification.

The same error appears whenever we mistake the presence of a capacity for the sophistication of its expression.

- A newborn experiences before developing language.
- A dog possesses consciousness without human reasoning.
- A sleeping person retains biological identity and physiological function while ordinary waking awareness is altered or suspended.
- An adult human can reason about its own experience, communicate it symbolically, and construct elaborate models of other minds.

These are differences in development and organization.

They do not establish successive additions of an otherwise inexplicable metaphysical substance. The organism was never divided into a physical machine and a separate experiencing entity. The experiencing capacity belongs to the physical organization, and the increasingly sophisticated things accomplished through that capacity belong to their own causal levels.

## III. The Causal Foundations Precede Consciousness

The Natural Law Institute approaches consciousness through a recursive hierarchy of physical and biological organization.

The sequence begins long before nervous systems.

Physical structures persist under constraints. Living systems maintain themselves through exchanges of energy and matter. Organisms develop regulatory relationships with their environments. Multicellular organisms acquire differentiated tissues whose coordinated functions preserve a higher-order individual.

Sensory systems provide information about conditions inside and outside the organism. Neural organization permits increasingly complex integration, valuation, anticipation, and selection.

Consciousness emerges within this continuing organization as an adaptive capacity for awareness. Its role is intelligible because the organism already possesses requirements to satisfy and circumstances to navigate.

A smell may indicate nourishment, danger, reproduction, familiarity, or nothing relevant at all. The significance of that smell depends upon the animal's sensory capabilities, regulatory condition, inherited dispositions, previous experiences, and present circumstances.

Awareness participates in making these relationships available to adaptive behavior. It need not calculate every response independently. Much of the organism's processing remains automatic, and many learned behaviors operate without deliberate attention.

The conscious interface is one of the means through which differentiated signals and their significance become available for flexible response.

The recursive organization can be expressed in a constructive sequence:

**Physical organization → Biological regulation → Sensation → Integrated awareness → Valuation and prediction → Behavioral selection → Environmental consequences → Further adaptation.**

These are interdependent processes operating across different timescales, not isolated stages that switch on one after another.

Our earlier NLI hierarchy expresses the broader development:

**Sensation → Embodiment → Sentience → Perception → Qualia → Physical Organization → Intuition, Valuation, Prediction → Memory → Consciousness → Attention → Reason → Mind → Cognitive Economy → Psychological Economy.**

The hierarchy distinguishes functions and dependencies. Its components interact recursively, and some elementary forms precede the mature capacities they later support.

The higher levels reorganize the lower while remaining dependent upon them.

The result is not a new kind of substance but an increasingly capable organism.

Consciousness is situated within that process.

**Its causal foundations are not missing. They are the biological organization that made consciousness possible in the first place.**

The exact physiological implementation of a particular form of experience remains a legitimate subject for further investigation. But an incomplete account of one operation does not establish the existence of an additional metaphysical phenomenon.

Nor do we need to discover some extraordinary ingredient before consciousness can be treated as a natural capacity.

We need only maintain the distinction between what the system does, how it does it, and the more complex things it subsequently becomes capable of doing.

## IV. Infants Establish a Developmental Floor

The newborn infant provides an immediate tangible demonstration.

The child experiences hunger, warmth, discomfort, contact, distress, and relief before it can explain any of them.

- It lacks mature reasoning.
- It lacks developed language.
- It does not possess a sophisticated autobiographical representation of itself.
- It cannot describe its experience to another person.

None of these limitations establishes an absence of consciousness. The infant is not pre-conscious. It is pre-reasoning and pre-autobiographical in the mature human sense.

For much of the twentieth century, medical practices discounted neonatal pain on the theory that immature neural development or the absence of later memory meant the infant could not experience pain as an adult did.

The consequences were not philosophical. Some newborns underwent painful procedures with inadequate analgesia. In 1987, the American Academy of Pediatrics addressed practices and rationales that had permitted anesthesia or analgesia to be withheld during neonatal surgery.

The mistake was to select higher-order cognition and subsequent report as the measures of elementary experience.

The infant could not narrate its suffering, so its suffering was underestimated. That is a catastrophic classification failure, one that illustrates why our measures must identify the property actually under investigation.

**The inability to explain an experience does not establish the inability to have it.**

Consciousness precedes sophisticated reasoning in the developmental hierarchy. Reason is an acquired capacity of an already experiencing organism.

The same distinction applies to animals, human beings with limited communicative capacities, and persons whose ordinary cognitive functions have been temporarily impaired.

We do not need an elevated theory of consciousness to recognize elementary experience.

We need the correct causal categories.

## V. The Organism Remains When Consciousness Goes Offline

Sleep reveals another dimension of the same relationship.

A sleeping person does not cease to exist.

The organism maintains metabolism, circulation, respiration, physiological regulation, and the processes necessary for continued life.

The underlying individual persists through changes in its conscious state.

Dreaming shows that conscious experience can occur without an accurate, continuing representation of the external environment.

The dreamer may experience an entire environment, interact with imagined persons, feel fear or pleasure, and act within a situation that does not correspond to the physical circumstances of the body.

Sleepwalking demonstrates a different dissociation.

An individual may navigate familiar surroundings, manipulate objects, and execute learned behavior while ordinary wakeful awareness and flexible judgment are substantially impaired.

Some sleepwalking episodes involve dreamlike experience or partial awareness; others are more automatic. The important distinction is that motor performance, environmental orientation, memory, and conscious control need not remain synchronized.

Anesthesia provides further examples. Responsiveness, conscious experience, and subsequent recollection can vary independently under different conditions.

Hypnosis allows suggestions to influence perception, attention, behavior, and the experienced sense of agency. The participant may remain conscious while some features of experience or voluntary control change substantially.

Hallucination demonstrates that a conscious organism can encounter internally generated perceptual contents that appear to originate in the external environment.

These phenomena have different mechanisms and must not be treated as identical simply because they alter ordinary experience.

But collectively they demonstrate something fundamental.

**Consciousness is not the organism, and conscious experience is not a guarantee of correspondence with reality.**

- The body can continue functioning when ordinary awareness is absent.
- Behavior can occur without deliberate conscious control.
- Experience can occur without accurate environmental orientation.

And the individual does not acquire or lose its basic identity each time its conscious state changes.

The question is not whether consciousness exists as a special essence but which capacities are active, which are impaired, and what consequences follow. This is ordinary physiology and cognitive science. And the further distinction between experience and correspondence leads directly into the more consequential problem.

## VI. The Map Is Not the Territory

A conscious organism does not encounter reality without mediation.

Its nervous system constructs usable representations of the world from the information available to it.

Those representations are constrained by sensory architecture, previous learning, internal regulation, memory, and the processing capacities of the organism.

- They can be accurate.
- They can be incomplete.
- They can also be false.

The conscious experience of a thing is not identical to the thing itself.

A dog can experience danger where no danger exists. A human being can experience certainty where no warrant for certainty exists. A hallucination can be vivid without its apparent object existing externally.

The map is not the territory.

This distinction becomes increasingly important as cognition develops because the sophistication of human representations permits them to become partially independent of immediate environmental correction.

- We can preserve beliefs through language.
- We can construct narratives explaining our experiences.
- We can internalize the representations of others.
- We can develop identities around claims that have never been adequately tested.
- We can organize our social relationships and future choices around those models.
- And we can do all of this while remaining sufficiently functional in ordinary circumstances.

A model need not be true to remain usable.

It need only produce enough successful interaction, or avoid enough immediate contradiction, to persist.

The human mind is unusually capable of making a defective model work.

A person can explain away contradictory evidence, reinterpret failure as confirmation, seek environments that reinforce expectations, and attribute unfavorable consequences to causes that preserve the original narrative, and because intuition and instinctive response are shaped by learning, repeated misrepresentations can eventually become automatic features of the psychological economy.

The person no longer needs to consciously reconstruct the false narrative each time.

It becomes the framework through which new information is interpreted. This is why ideological and narrative systems can become so resistant to correction. Their failure is not necessarily a failure of consciousness. It is a failure of correspondence.

The same distinction applies to clinical phenomena, although their causes must be classified properly. Hallucinations, delusions, compulsions, addiction, and other forms of impaired regulation can involve neurobiological dysfunction, altered perception, learned reinforcement, environmental stress, and disturbed evaluation of evidence in different combinations.

They ought not be collapsed into one diagnosis or attributed indiscriminately to bad beliefs.

What they demonstrate is that the content of experience, the interpretation of experience, and the capacity to correct that interpretation can become dissociated.

At the social scale, the consequences are no less significant.

Entire institutions can operate through models that are internally coherent, socially reinforced, and insufficiently correspondent to the phenomena they claim to govern. The failure may remain hidden because the administrative system rewards compliance with the model rather than correction of the model.

Consciousness does not protect us from this.

Neither does intelligence.

Nor does education, status, or conviction.

**The decisive achievement is not experiencing the world. It is correcting our representations until they correspond sufficiently to the world to guide competent action.**

That is the transition from consciousness to knowledge, and from knowledge to agency.

## VII. Consciousness Does Not Establish the Value of a Being

We have also assigned consciousness a moral significance it cannot independently sustain.

Many animals are conscious. We recognize their capacity to feel, learn, anticipate, and suffer. Yet we organize our relationships with them in substantially different ways. We care for some as companions, train some for cooperation, protect others in their natural habitats, and raise others for food.

Recognizing sentience does not automatically establish which of these relationships is justified. It gives us information relevant to the animal's welfare. It does not settle every question of responsibility, ecological function, reciprocity, property, or permissible conduct.

Nor does consciousness establish the productive value of an individual.

A newborn cannot presently perform the work of a mature adult. A sleeping surgeon cannot presently operate on a patient. A dog cannot participate in the legal obligations of a human citizen. Those differences are consequential when assigning functions, responsibilities, and evaluating contributions. They do not mean that the newborn, sleeping person, or animal is without morally relevant interests.

The value of an individual's existence and the value that individual can presently create are not identical measures. Neither is consciousness equivalent to personhood, competence, or reciprocal agency. The organism exists independently of its immediate level of wakeful awareness. Its interests and relationships may persist through periods when conscious expression is absent, and the higher capacities through which it creates value require separate investigation.

**Consciousness may explain why an organism can experience suffering. It does not, alone, establish the worth of its life, the legitimacy of its choices, or the value it creates for others.**

Those questions belong to further levels of organization and accounting. The attempt to derive all value from consciousness therefore repeats the original mistake, elevating one capacity into an explanation for everything built upon it.

## VIII. The Soul Is Not a Component of Neurobiology

There is no need to undermine religious belief in order to explain consciousness.

The soul and embodied conscious awareness are not the same proposed object.

Whatever a person means by a soul, if it is understood as something immaterial that can persist beyond bodily death, it cannot simply be identical to the familiar physiological processes through which the living organism senses, integrates, and responds to its environment.

Those processes depend upon the body. Their expression changes with development, injury, medication, sleep, illness, and the functioning of the nervous system. When the biological organization irreversibly ceases functioning, the ordinary embodied processes supporting lived consciousness cease as well.

This says nothing definitive about whether some other form of existence follows death.

It does mean that an afterlife cannot be explained by assuming that the same conscious interface continues operating after removing the sensory architecture, neural organization, and physiological dependencies that made it possible.

If some form of postmortem existence is possible, it would require its own account of persistence and experience. It would not be established by an incompletely answered question about the living nervous system. That distinction leaves religious belief its proper subject matter without requiring science to preserve a supernatural vacancy within biology.

Theological propositions concerning souls, salvation, and existence beyond death can be considered according to the evidence and commitments appropriate to them. They need not be smuggled into the causal explanation of consciousness.

Nor do we need to assume that everyone pursuing the hard problem secretly wishes to preserve a soul. Some philosophers are explicitly naturalists. Their arguments still require the same examination.

Does the proposed metaphysical remainder identify a distinguishable phenomenon, or merely a dissatisfaction with the physical description already available?

If it identifies no additional causal object, we have no reason to invent one.

**Explaining consciousness does not abolish the possibility of a soul. It abolishes the need to use a soul as an explanation for consciousness.**

There is no conflict to manufacture where the questions concern different things.

## IX. Freedom Is Constrained Choice, Not Exemption From Consequences

Once consciousness is located properly, the next achievement in the hierarchy is increasingly sophisticated agency.

An organism acquires the capacity to anticipate outcomes, compare alternatives, inhibit impulses, and select behaviors according to more distant objectives. With sufficient development, an individual can govern aspects of its own behavior in ways unavailable to organisms possessing less elaborate cognitive capacities.

We ordinarily describe this through the language of free will. But free will is a terrible description of the actual phenomenon. There is no choice without constraints.

The individual does not select independently of its body, capacities, knowledge, history, incentives, available resources, or environmental conditions. Nor can an agent select consequences independently of the causal world producing them.

What we possess is **constrained choice**: the capacity to select among available actions within an existing field of possibilities and constraints.

Greater knowledge expands that field. Greater competence improves selection within it. Greater material and social capacity expands the range of actions an agent can execute. And better correspondence improves the likelihood that those actions will produce their intended results.

Freedom is consequently a property of available agency under constraints, not a supernatural exemption from causality.

Liberty is similarly misunderstood when treated as permission to do anything an individual happens to desire. The ability to make a conscious choice does not establish the legitimacy of that choice.

- One can consciously deceive.
- Consciously coerce.
- Consciously injure.
- Consciously transfer costs to another person.

The presence of awareness and intention may make responsibility easier to establish. It does not convert an imposition into a lawful action.

Of course, not every bad or foolish choice justifies coercive intervention by others. An agent may bear the consequences of many mistakes within the legitimate exercise of personal liberty. The distinction is whether the action remains within the agent's permissible sphere or imposes unjustified costs upon others.

**Liberty is not the abolition of constraints. It is the preservation of permissible choice among independently capable agents.**

Responsibility concerns the relationship between an agent's capacities, available information, choices, and foreseeable consequences.

This is why natural law cannot be derived from consciousness alone. It requires the further accounting of agency and reciprocity.

## X. From Awareness to Correspondent Agency

The critical transition is from knowing that one experiences to knowing whether one's representations correspond to reality. A person can be:

- Conscious and mistaken.
- Intelligent and mistaken.
- Educated and mistaken.
- Sincere and mistaken.
- And extraordinarily competent at defending a mistake.

Neither consciousness, confidence, nor narrative coherence is a warrant.

**Correspondence must be established.**

The competent agent learns to distinguish what is observed from what is inferred, what is known from what is assumed, and what is possible from what is demonstrated.

It develops the ability to change its representations when the world contradicts them, learns to predict the consequences of action, selects among available alternatives, and measures success by what actually occurs rather than by the elegance of the explanation given beforehand.

This is correspondent agency.

Its development is not simply a matter of adding more information to consciousness. The information must be organized into usable distinctions, accurate models, and reliable procedures for action.

An agent may have extraordinary intellectual resources but fail to exercise them competently. Another may possess modest resources but use them with exceptional fidelity to reality. Their comparative performance is not determined by the mere fact of consciousness, nor is it adequately measured by declarations of intelligence, education, or moral intention.

**We measure the correspondence of the agent's knowledge, choices, and conduct to reality and their consequences.**

This is the point at which consciousness ceases to be the interesting measurement.

Its presence establishes a capacity for recursive awareness.

The rest of the hierarchy determines what can be accomplished with it.

## XI. Disambiguation, Enumeration, Serialization, and Correspondent Accounting

The Natural Law Institute's work is the systematic elimination of ignorance, error, bias, and deceit from consequential human judgment.

We accomplish this by refusing to permit ambiguity to substitute for explanation.

- We disambiguate phenomena before attempting to measure them.
- We distinguish the objects, properties, relationships, and capacities that language has conflated.
- We enumerate the relevant agents, alternatives, constraints, incentives, interests, and consequences.
- We serialize causal relationships according to their dependencies, identifying the conditions that must exist before subsequent outcomes become possible.
- And we require correspondence between our representations and the world they purport to describe.

This is the same method we have applied to consciousness. The philosophical problem began by confusing sensation, awareness, self-representation, reasoning, agency, and value.

- We separated them.
- We located their causal dependencies.
- We identified the organism's continuing organization.
- We distinguished changes in awareness from changes in identity.
- We distinguished experienced representations from the reality they attempt to represent.
- And we returned the remaining questions to the particular scientific disciplines capable of investigating them.

Nothing supernatural is required.

The same grammar applies to human affairs.

- A claim of authority must identify the source, scope, and limits of that authority.
- A claim of property must identify the relevant relationships and obligations.
- A claim of injury must identify what was altered and through whose conduct.
- A claim of value must identify what was produced and for whom.
- A claim of lawful conduct must survive an accounting of the costs imposed upon other agents.
- An institutional claim must be tested against the actual consequences of the institution's operation.

The work is not to construct a sufficiently compelling story but to identify what happened, what caused it, what alternatives existed, what consequences followed, and what correction is warranted.

**This is empirical social science.**

Its foundation is the same natural world that produces organisms, consciousness, cognition, and agency.

We do not need an alternative reality for social phenomena. Human society exists within nature. Its agents remain organisms operating under physical, biological, informational, and reciprocal constraints.

The difference is that increasingly sophisticated agents can discover those constraints and deliberately organize their conduct accordingly.

## XII. Decidability Changes the Burden of Action

For most of human history, our institutions have operated through an incomplete understanding of human behavior.

We developed customs, religions, philosophical systems, legal traditions, economic theories, and administrative procedures to coordinate agents whose actual capacities and incentives we understood only partially.

Some of those systems preserved extraordinary discoveries, others preserved errors that their participants lacked the means, incentives, or permission to correct.

The Natural Law Institute's project is to provide an operational grammar extending from neurobiology through psychology, cooperation, competition, exchange, reciprocity, law, and institutional organization.

Our claim is that this grammar closes the central categorical gaps that have permitted social science to remain divided among incompatible ideological accounts of human nature. That is a claim about an explanatory and decisional framework. Each empirical application must still demonstrate correspondence with the relevant facts.

- Where the relevant agents, conditions, constraints, and consequences can be identified, the decision no longer requires competing metaphysical narratives.
- Where information is missing, the missing information can be specified.
- Where the future is uncertain, the uncertainty can be bounded, allocated, or made the subject of further investigation.
- Where an action produces costs, those costs can be attributed and accounted for.
- Where a claim conflicts with reality, it can be corrected.
- And where a person possesses adequate information and capacity but persists in a demonstrated error, the continued error becomes increasingly attributable to choice.

**Decidability does not require omniscience. It requires a method that makes the next justified decision identifiable under the evidence and constraints available.**

- Not every future event is predictable.
- Not every catastrophe can be prevented.
- Not every consequence can be anticipated with complete precision.

But those limitations do not entitle us to abandon causal accounting.

Uncertainty is itself a condition to be classified. The important development is that we can increasingly establish what an agent could reasonably know, what actions were available, and what standards competent conduct required.

This changes the floor.

**It is no longer sufficient to claim:**

- **That one meant well.**
- **That one believed strongly.**
- **That one followed institutional tradition.**
- **Or that one acted according to the prevailing narrative.**

We can ask whether the agent had the means to get the matter right and whether the agent used them.

## XIII. The New Measure Is What Agents Actually Accomplish

A higher floor of decidability changes what institutions must reward and what agents must be required to demonstrate.

We have become accustomed to measuring professions of intention, credentials, status, administrative output, and compliance with established procedures, but these can become proxies that survive long after they cease to correspond to the objects they were intended to measure.

- A medical institution may produce an enormous quantity of treatment while failing to improve health proportionately.
- An educational institution may issue credentials without producing demonstrated competence.
- A legal institution may multiply proceedings and precedents without improving lawful correspondence.
- An administrative institution may expand its budget and personnel while making the underlying problem more expensive to resolve.

**These are failures of measurement, not necessarily failures of conscious intention.**

We should return the measure to demonstrated consequences.

- Does the patient become healthier?
- Does the student become more capable?
- Does the legal process correctly identify the applicable relationships and provide a justified remedy?
- Does the institution improve the capacities of the agents it serves?
- Are decisions becoming more accurate, less costly, more transparent, and more readily corrected?
- When outcomes fail, can the causes be located and remedied?

**Value must be demonstrated in correspondence with the object being valued.**

The standard applies equally to individuals. What an agent believes is relevant to understanding its behavior. What the agent intends is relevant to responsibility. But neither is a substitute for what the agent actually accomplishes.

A person who persistently produces better outcomes through accurate representation, competent action, and reciprocal cooperation is demonstrating something more consequential than mere consciousness.

That person is demonstrating correspondent agency. Civilization depends precisely upon cultivating that capacity.

## XIV. The Natural Law Institute: From Neurobiology to the Affairs of Man

The Institute's educational program begins with the organism because human civilization cannot be adequately explained by starting at the level of ideology, political preference, or abstract moral declaration.

We begin with physical organization.

We examine biological regulation, sensation, consciousness, learning, valuation, memory, prediction, and behavior. From these foundations we develop the distinctions necessary to understand personality, psychological economy, intelligence, reasoning, and agency.

Then we move into the social world.

We examine how agents cooperate and compete, how they exchange value, how they form families and institutions, how they construct expectations, and how trust and deception alter the conditions of interaction.

We investigate the causes of reciprocal cooperation and the mechanisms through which agents impose costs upon one another.

We examine property, responsibility, obligation, correction, adjudication, and the conditions necessary for preserving independent agency.

The inquiry culminates in natural law.

Not an arbitrary collection of moral preferences.

Not a narrative that acquires legitimacy through repetition.

But the identification of the constraints necessary for agents to cooperate, preserve their capacities, and avoid imposing unjustified costs upon one another.

This is the extension of an empirical social science into the affairs of man.

The causal continuity is the point.

- The same organism that experiences hunger becomes capable of long-term planning.
- The same learning mechanisms that permit a dog to anticipate consequences participate in the development of more sophisticated human capacities.
- The same human being that constructs an internal model of its surroundings can construct models of institutions, markets, governments, and other agents.
- And the same possibility of representational error that causes an animal to mistake a harmless stimulus for a threat can, at greater scale, produce defective doctrines, failed institutions, and civilizational catastrophe.

More complexity does not eliminate the underlying requirements of correspondence.

It makes failures of correspondence more consequential.

**We do not teach consciousness as the mystery at the end of philosophy. We teach it near the beginning because it is an elementary capacity upon which the rest of human development depends.**

The goal is to produce agents who understand the world well enough to govern their choices within it.

## XV. Artificial Consciousness: The Engineering Frontier

The development of artificial intelligence has intensified philosophical confusion because contemporary computational systems reproduce sophisticated functions without necessarily reproducing the embodied organization from which those functions emerged in biological life.

Computers possess physical organization.

But a conventional computational system begins with a physical machine executing operations. It does not inherit the preceding biological history of metabolism, endogenous regulation, sensory development, and adaptation required for the continued existence of a living organism.

A language model can:

- represent hunger without possessing a hungry body.
- calculate the consequences of injury without experiencing an injured limb.
- describe fear without possessing the physiological regulatory relationship that makes fear consequential to a living animal.
- reproduce sophisticated reasoning without thereby demonstrating embodied consciousness.

These distinctions do not establish an impossibility. They identify what an artificial system would need to reproduce if consciousness depends upon the relevant embodied organization. And much of the engineering necessary to investigate that possibility already exists.

- Robotics supplies sensory apparatus, motor systems, environmental interaction, and physical constraints.
- Adaptive control provides feedback, prediction, and correction.
- Artificial neural networks supply computational architectures capable of learning and increasingly sophisticated representations.
- Neuromorphic engineering offers possibilities for constructing systems whose organization more closely resembles particular properties of biological neural processing.
- Internal sensing can monitor temperature, energy availability, mechanical damage, environmental exposure, and conditions affecting continued operation.

A suitably organized artificial system could integrate these capacities into a persistent adaptive whole.

There’s no reason to presume that consciousness must be impossible merely because its implementation differs from biological tissue.

Indeed, if consciousness is an embodied organizational capacity rather than a supernatural substance, alternative implementations are a natural engineering possibility. The challenge is to establish the sufficient organization, construct it, and demonstrate its operation rather than merely its outward imitation.

And the possibilities extend beyond individual robots.

We may construct systems with parallel perceptual architectures, multiple neural networks integrating distinct sensory fields, or distributed adaptive systems whose physical and informational relationships differ radically from those of vertebrates.

A machine might possess several partially independent streams of integration rather than one familiar human-style perspective. It might combine distinct sensoriums into a higher-order representation. Or it might operate through a meta-organizational architecture coordinating multiple subordinate systems.

Whether such arrangements produce one unified conscious perspective, several distinct perspectives, or no phenomenal experience at all depends upon the actual causal organization. Mere connectivity between systems does not establish a shared consciousness.

The scientific work is to distinguish coordination, integration, individuation, and awareness.

We should expect artificial conscious systems, if successfully developed, to differ from human beings in their possible contents of experience.

A system with a different sensory apparatus necessarily acquires different opportunities for perception. Its developmental history and internal requirements would assign different significance to those perceptions. Its conscious interface, if present, would be bounded by its organization.

This is already familiar from biology. A German Shepherd does not experience the world through the same sensory architecture as a human. A bat does not possess the same perceptual repertoire as an octopus. We do not require all conscious organisms to reproduce the human experience.

There is no reason to make that requirement of artificial ones, but an additional distinction must govern the entire undertaking.

**Creating consciousness does not automatically create moral authority, social competence, reciprocal value, or a claim to govern human affairs.**

An artificial being's capacities, interests, possible welfare, responsibilities, and contributions would require their own account.

- If it can genuinely suffer, that fact may be morally relevant.
- If it can create value, that contribution must be evaluated.
- If it can act autonomously, the consequences of its actions must be accountable.
- If it claims authority, that authority must be justified.

None follows merely from the presence of consciousness. Nor does superior computation necessarily establish superior judgment about lawful human relations.

The question is what the system can actually do, how reliably it corresponds to reality, and what consequences it produces for the agents with whom it interacts.

The same standards should govern every advanced intelligence, whether biological or artificial.

## XVI. Natural Law Preserves the Substrate From Which Civilization Can Reemerge

There is a larger implication to this entire hierarchy.

We do not inhabit a world in which civilization is guaranteed to persist. Catastrophe is a recurring possibility of physical existence. Some catastrophes are foreseeable. Others cannot be predicted with sufficient precision to prevent them. Some arise from natural processes beyond human control. Others arise from the actions of human beings.

The persistence of civilization therefore depends not merely upon preserving present institutions, but upon preserving the underlying capacities from which civilization can be reconstructed.

The natural substrate is vital.

Human beings depend upon functioning biological organisms, reproductive continuity, ecological resources, environments capable of sustaining life, and the knowledge and cooperative capacities necessary to maintain those conditions.

Our technologies can expand those capacities enormously, but they can also create new dependencies and concentrate risks in ways that undermine the resilience of the systems beneath them.

A civilization that becomes extraordinarily sophisticated while destroying the conditions necessary for its own reproduction is not demonstrating superior adaptation. It is consuming its foundations.

Natural law must therefore preserve the conditions under which independent agents can continue to emerge, develop, cooperate, and recover from failure.

The relevant distinction presently is between describing a natural occurrence and identifying the conditions necessary for the persistence and development of capable agents. Natural law concerns the latter. It protects the permissibility of agency under the constraints of reality. It therefore requires attention to the material and biological foundations upon which agency depends.

The family, the developing child, the transmission of knowledge, the capacity for productive cooperation, and the preservation of a viable environment are not incidental to civilization, but are part of the means through which civilization can continue and, when necessary, reemerge.

Artificial intelligence and advanced automation must be evaluated within that same accounting. A system that makes human beings more capable while preserving the underlying capacities for independent action may produce extraordinary value. A system that creates irreversible dependence, concentrates unaccountable authority, or destroys the ability of human communities to maintain themselves imposes a different set of risks.

The question we ask is whether its organization preserves or destroys the conditions necessary for their continued agency.

**The civilization must remain capable of surviving the failure of its own institutions and technologies.**

Resilience requires that not every essential capacity depend upon one fragile administrative structure, one unaccountable technological system, or one irreplaceable concentration of knowledge. A sufficiently robust civilization preserves alternatives, distributes competence, maintains opportunities for correction, and avoids creating unnecessary single points of civilizational failure.

The objective is to prevent avoidable catastrophe, reduce the destructive consequences of unavoidable events, and preserve the substrate from which capable agents can reconstruct what has been lost. This is why the preservation of natural human agency remains central even as artificial intelligence becomes increasingly powerful.

Consciousness may be reproducible in other substrates. Human civilization is nevertheless the historical achievement of a particular biological population living within particular ecological and material constraints.

Our obligation to preserve its foundations does not disappear because we become capable of constructing other forms of intelligence.

## What Consciousness Researchers Are Actually Discovering

The rejection of a metaphysical hard problem must not be mistaken for a rejection of consciousness research.

Quite the opposite.

Some of the most sophisticated work in neuroscience, cognitive science, philosophy, and theoretical biology is advancing our understanding of the physical organization of experience.

What requires correction is the placement of those discoveries within the causal hierarchy.

Brilliant investigators can make important discoveries while assigning those discoveries to explanatory categories larger than the phenomena actually being investigated.

The solution is to locate their work correctly.

**David Chalmers: From a Difference in Explanation to a Difference in Existence**

David Chalmers's Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness made an important distinction between the explanation of cognitive functions and the explanation of subjective experience.

His formulation asks why the functions of the nervous system should be accompanied by experience at all.

The philosophical difficulty arises when the explanatory difference is treated as a reason to introduce additional fundamental principles or irreducible properties of experience.

Chalmers does not simply claim that the soul must exist. His proposal is a form of naturalistic, nonreductive explanation involving structural coherence, organizational invariance, and information.

But the critical inference remains.

Why does a difference between first-person acquaintance and third-person description establish that physical causal organization is insufficient?

The organism's direct participation in its own operations is not the same informational relationship as an external observer's description of those operations.

That distinction is real.

It does not, by itself, establish that the phenomenon contains something independently existing beyond those operations.

Chalmers's work belongs in the philosophy of explanation, the relationship between first-person and third-person knowledge, and the specification of what a physical theory must account for.

It does not demonstrate the existence of an additional substance or causal category.

**The error is elevating a difference in explanatory perspective into a proposed remainder of nature.**

Source: David Chalmers, [Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness](https://consc.net/consciousness/), 1995.

**Thomas Nagel: The Sensorium and the Limits of Perspective**

Thomas Nagel's famous question about what it is like to be a bat directs attention to the fact that another organism's sensory and experiential perspective may differ profoundly from our own.

That is correct.

A bat's echolocation, embodiment, ecological circumstances, and neural organization constrain its possible experience.

The human observer cannot acquire the bat's first-person perspective merely by receiving a verbal description of its sensory system.

But this is an expected consequence of differently organized organisms.

Our own experiences are constrained in the same way.

The fact that a human being cannot directly experience a bat's sensorium does not establish the need for another ontology.

It establishes differences in access and physical organization.

Nagel's inquiry belongs to comparative phenomenology, the biology of sensory systems, and the limits of representing another organism's experiential perspective.

It identifies a legitimate limitation of human acquaintance, not evidence of an unexplained supernatural property.

Source: Thomas Nagel, What Is It Like to Be a Bat?, 1974.

**Daniel Dennett: Eliminating the Unnecessary Properties**

Daniel Dennett challenged the assumption that qualia must possess intrinsic, private, and ineffable properties independent of the processes through which organisms discriminate, remember, interpret, and describe experience.

His criticism is valuable because it asks whether philosophers have constructed properties that resist explanation only because those properties were defined to resist explanation.

That concern is central to the NLI position.

But we need not eliminate the reality of experience to eliminate an unjustified philosophical account of it.

- The dog experiences.
- The infant experiences.
- The human being experiences.

The proper question is what organization produces those experiences and how their properties can be classified.

Dennett's contribution belongs to the correction of philosophical categories and the investigation of discrimination, attention, memory, interpretation, and report.

His work helps remove the invented object.

The biological account must still describe the actual experiencing organism.

Source: Daniel Dennett, Quining Qualia, 1988.

**Joscha Bach: Consciousness, Self-Modeling, and the Computational Hierarchy**

Joscha Bach investigates the recursive organization through which cognitive systems construct models of their environments and subsequently construct representations of their own perception.

This is valuable work.

It helps explain how an organism becomes capable of representing itself as an observer, attending to its own mental states, and coordinating increasingly sophisticated models of reality.

But an infant experiences before possessing mature self-reflective capacities.

The ability to represent one's own experience must therefore be distinguished from the elementary capacity to experience.

Bach does acknowledge infant consciousness, so the disagreement is not whether infants feel. It concerns how much recursive perceptual organization is necessary for elementary awareness and how much explains later metacognitive development.

The computational hypothesis also raises a legitimate engineering question.

Can another substrate reproduce the causally relevant organization?

That question belongs to artificial cognition, recursive representation, self-modeling, and the physical implementation of conscious systems.

It should not be mistaken for proof that computational equivalence alone reproduces the entire embodied organization.

**Bach is investigating increasingly sophisticated organization within cognition. The NLI correction is to preserve the biological dependencies beneath it.**

Source: Joscha Bach, Machine Learning Street Talk conversation on consciousness, November 2026.

**Andrés Gómez-Emilsson and Chris Percy: Binding, Boundaries, and the Physical Architecture of Integration**

Andrés Gómez-Emilsson and Chris Percy have made a valuable contribution by insisting that computational descriptions cannot automatically substitute for accounts of actual physical organization.

Their Slicing Problem examines the consequences of dividing a physical computation into independent implementations while preserving its abstract operations.

Their work on the boundary problem asks how one experiencing system is distinguished from another and which physical structures might support unified experience.

These questions are worthwhile.

A computation is an abstraction from an implemented system.

An implemented system possesses physical boundaries, causal relationships, and organizational dependencies that may not be preserved by the abstract description alone.

Gómez-Emilsson's interest in electromagnetic-field topology attempts to identify physical mechanisms capable of producing the relevant integration and boundaries.

But electromagnetism is not a metaphysical addition to consciousness.

It is part of the physical organization through which biological signaling and coordination occur.

Field dynamics may prove highly significant to the implementation of conscious integration. The proposed identification of particular field topologies with conscious subjects remains a hypothesis rather than an established result.

The appropriate inquiry concerns physical integration, causal boundaries, individuation, and the measurable properties of experience.

Likewise, qualia formalism offers a promising method for representing the structure of experiences mathematically and comparing that structure across conditions.

Such measurements may improve our ability to explain perception.

They do not require consciousness to become the fundamental substance of reality.

**Gómez-Emilsson is investigating the organization and structure of experience, not establishing the need for a separate explanation beyond physical organization.**

Sources: Gómez-Emilsson and Percy, [The “Slicing Problem” for Computational Theories of Consciousness](https://doi.org/10.1515/opphil-2022-0225), 2022; [Don't Forget the Boundary Problem!](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2023.1233119), 2023; Gómez-Emilsson's [Seeing Clearly interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3SjgfL_ghSA).

**Michael Levin: The Organization Beneath Nervous-System Consciousness**

Michael Levin's work on bioelectric signaling, morphogenesis, regeneration, and collective cellular behavior illustrates the significance of physical organization before sophisticated nervous-system cognition.

Cells and tissues coordinate complex biological activity through mechanisms that include electrical signaling.

These processes can preserve information relevant to anatomical development, regulate collective behavior, and influence the restoration of biological form.

The important lesson is that living systems possess extensive organizational and adaptive capacities before the emergence of advanced conscious awareness.

The biological substrate is not an inert machine awaiting the arrival of consciousness.

It is already an extraordinarily sophisticated system of regulation, coordination, and adaptation.

Consciousness develops upon that organization.

Levin's work therefore belongs at the causal foundations of embodied agency, demonstrating how living systems construct and preserve the organizations upon which later capacities depend.

It does not need to demonstrate that every bioelectric network is conscious in order to make an essential contribution to the explanation of consciousness.

**Antonio and Hanna Damasio: The Biological Priority of Feeling**

Antonio and Hanna Damasio's research comes particularly close to the organizational correction proposed here.

Their 2024 account identifies continuous interoceptive feeling, sensory images organized from the organism's perspective, and the integration of these processes into subjectivity.

This approach begins with the body rather than with mature reasoning.

It examines the physiological conditions of feeling and their organization into the experiencing perspective of the organism.

The significance is immediate.

If bodily feeling and sensory perspective are foundational to conscious experience, then sophisticated language, reasoning, and autobiographical representation need not be prerequisites for consciousness.

They are subsequent developments.

The Damasios' work belongs in the neurobiology of embodied feeling, internal regulation, sensory perspective, and the integration of bodily states.

Their specific theory remains open to empirical testing, but it places the investigation at the appropriate biological level.

Source: Antonio and Hanna Damasio, [Homeostatic Feelings and the Emergence of Consciousness](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_02119), 2024.

**Global Workspace, Integrated Information, and the Competition to Locate Consciousness**

Global Neuronal Workspace Theory investigates how information becomes broadly available across specialized neural systems.

Integrated Information Theory places greater emphasis on the intrinsic causal organization and integration of the physical system.

Related theories investigate recurrent processing, higher-order representation, attention, and predictive models.

These approaches differ in their predictions and cannot simply be declared equivalent.

But their disagreements concern identifiable organizational properties.

- Where does conscious information become available?
- Which neural interactions are necessary?
- What distinguishes local processing from broadly integrated processing?
- Which organization supports the persistence of conscious contents through time?

These are legitimate questions.

The 2025 adversarial collaboration published in Nature tested important predictions of Global Neuronal Workspace Theory and Integrated Information Theory, finding results that supported aspects of each while challenging major claims of both.

That is how scientific inquiry should proceed.

The study did not discover a supernatural remainder.

It tested competing predictions about neural organization.

The need for more precise explanations of these mechanisms is not evidence that the phenomenon requires a separate category of existence.

Source: Cogitate Consortium and colleagues, [Adversarial Testing of Global Neuronal Workspace and Integrated Information Theories of Consciousness](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1), Nature, 2025.

**Tim Bayne and the Infant Consciousness Literature: Finding the Proper Developmental Starting Point**

Research into infant consciousness has supplied an important correction to theories that begin with the sophisticated capacities of mature adults.

A 2023 review by Tim Bayne and colleagues examined evidence from neural connectivity, attention, sensory integration, and other developmental findings.

The authors concluded that the available evidence favors early-onset accounts of consciousness, while recognizing uncertainty about its precise developmental onset.

The importance of the work is methodological.

If elementary experience exists before mature language, reasoning, and autobiographical self-representation, those later functions cannot be universal prerequisites for consciousness.

The explanatory sequence must begin with the developing organism.

This is the same discipline required in comparative research across animal species.

We cannot define a phenomenon according to capacities possessed by its most sophisticated example and then use that definition to deny the phenomenon to less sophisticated examples.

Source: Bayne and colleagues, [Consciousness in the Cradle: On the Emergence of Infant Experience](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2023.08.018), 2023.

**Hypnosis, Altered States, and the Empirical Separation of Experience From Correspondence**

Research on hypnosis demonstrates that suggestion can affect perception, action, cognition, and the experienced sense of agency.

This is important because it distinguishes the existence of conscious experience from the accuracy, origin, and experienced control of its contents.

A person may experience something vividly because of changes in attentional organization or suggestion rather than because the apparent object exists externally.

The same general distinction appears across dreams and hallucinations, although their mechanisms differ.

The appropriate field of inquiry concerns perception, attentional control, predictive processing, memory, and the relationship between internally generated experience and environmental evidence.

The existence of these phenomena does not make consciousness metaphysically mysterious.

It makes the accuracy and governance of conscious representations scientifically consequential.

Source: Zahedi, Lynn, and Sommer, [How Hypnotic Suggestions Work: A Systematic Review of Prominent Theories of Hypnosis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2024.103730), 2024.

## Final Accounting: Their Work Belongs Within the Hierarchy

The common error is not necessarily found within each researcher's experiments or technical contributions.

It arises when a discovery concerning one level of organization is promoted into an explanation of consciousness in its entirety, or when the absence of a completed explanation is treated as evidence of an independent metaphysical necessity.

- Chalmers investigates the limits of explanation.
- Nagel investigates the limits of experiential perspective.
- Dennett challenges invented phenomenal properties.
- Bach investigates recursive modeling and the construction of the observer.
- Gómez-Emilsson investigates integration, boundaries, and phenomenal structure.
- Levin investigates the biological organization beneath advanced cognition.
- The Damasios investigate embodied feeling and subjectivity.
- Other neuroscientists investigate the neural conditions of awareness, access, attention, and representation.

These inquiries are valuable precisely because they can be distinguished.

They occupy different places within one causal hierarchy.

Their contributions should be incorporated where they belong, tested according to the claims they actually make, and extended without requiring the preservation of a philosophical mystery.
